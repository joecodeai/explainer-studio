# Stage 1: Research & Story. "The Island That Mailed Letters Into the Sea" (St Kilda)
*21 Nov 2026. Target runtime is 11–12 minutes: about 1,750 narration words plus silent cinematic beats. 16:9.*

## Why this topic (and not the others)
- **Enough real story for 11–12 minutes without padding.** It covers 53 years, from 1877 to 1930, with a 2020 payoff. It has survival, humour, a WWI attack, an emotional climax and a twist ending.
- **The angle (corrected 21 Nov).** Several long YouTube videos cover St Kilda's 1930 evacuation as a sad travelogue. The mailboats **have** been covered: there's BBC One Show coverage (2015), a philately video, a small St Kilda Mailboat channel, Shorts and news reports of the 2020 Norway find. What I found no sign of is a long-form *story* video that uses the mailboats as its spine. That's our angle: a hook at the start, a turning point in the middle, a payoff at the end.
- **To do in Stage 2:** replace Wikipedia-only facts with primary or official sources:
  - Sands, *Chambers's Journal* 1877
  - NTS
  - The Times, 30 Aug 1930
  - The Scotsman (2010)
  - The kilda.org.uk evacuation account
- **Very cinematic.** Huge Atlantic waves, 400 m sea cliffs, men on ropes catching puffins, a tiny boat crossing the ocean, a German U-boat in the bay, a whole village leaving by ship.
- **Why not the 1904 marathon:** I re-checked it. It's now crowded on YouTube, with 10+ Shorts and 4+ long videos, including the 2023–24 "real winner" twist. My earlier slate called it an open lane, and that was wrong. Olympic art medals are open but visually static: people painting, judges judging.

## A. Video concept
**Title options:**
1. **This Island Mailed Letters by Throwing Them Into the Sea**
2. **Four Kids Found a Tiny Boat on an Arctic Beach. Inside Were Letters.**
3. **The Island Where Every Ship Brought a Cold**

**Central storyline:**
- In 2020, children in Arctic Norway find a tiny wooden boat with dry postcards inside.
- To explain how it got there, we go back to St Kilda. It was the most isolated community in Britain: no post, no doctor, no shop, and 41 miles of open Atlantic to the nearest islands. The islanders lived on seabirds and talked to the world by throwing boats into the sea.
- The story runs through a shipwreck rescue (1877), a famine plea from a 14-year-old (1885), a German submarine (1918) and the day the islanders voted to leave forever (1930).
- Then it lands back on that Norwegian beach.

**Target audience:** curious 18–45 viewers of Oversimplified, Sam O'Nella, BBC Ideas, Half as Interesting, and remote-places and "how people lived" content. UK, US, Scandinavia.

**Why it's interesting:** "mail delivered by the ocean" is an instant curiosity gap. It's real, documented, and almost no one has heard of it.

**Thumbnail concept:**
- A tiny hand-carved wooden boat with a little sail and a sheepskin float, riding a massive dark wave.
- An envelope sticks out of a small hatch.
- In the corner is a lone island cliff.
- Two-word text: **"IT ARRIVED."**

## Format (how Ryan & Maya fit)
- **Maya narrates** the whole documentary (approved Qwen design "I" voice, conversational and dry).
- **About 85% is cinematic historical scenes** with characters acting out exactly what's being said.
- **Ryan appears in 5–6 short host cutaways** (5–10 s each, our existing studio look). He's the viewer's voice: "Wait, they ate *what*?" Maya answers over the next cinematic scene. These are the humour breaks and keep the channel's identity.
- No moral ending. It ends on the Norway payoff and one dry final line.

| 5 | **The first "mail": 1877** | 5:45–7:15 | Journalist John Sands is stuck on the island for the winter. An Austrian ship, the *Peti Dubrovacki*, is wrecked and 9 sailors are marooned. Sands ties a message to a lifebuoy from the wreck and throws it into the sea. **9 days later it washes up in Birsay, Orkney.** A rescue is arranged. | Storm wreck, sailors on rocks, Sands writing by candle, lifebuoy into the waves, map of the drift, Orkney beach | Rising tension, then relief. **Ryan cutaway #3:** "Ocean Prime delivery" |
| 6 | **Turning point: 1885, the boy and the boats** | 7:15–8:30 | A storm ruins the island's food stores and the islanders face starvation. 14-year-old **Alexander Ferguson** has heard about Sands's trick. The islanders adopt the mailboat for good: a carved wooden boat, a sheepskin bladder float, the letter in a tin. **About two-thirds of launched messages were found, in Scotland or Norway.** | Kid carving boats, launching 5 in a row, one surviving a wave, newspaper headline, relief boat | The emotional high point of the story |

| 8 | **Climax: 1930, the vote to leave** | 9:30–11:00 | After the war the young men leave: 73 people in 1920, 37 in 1928. On **29 August 1930**, HMS *Harebell* takes the last **36** people away. Tradition: an open Bible and a small pile of oats in each house, doors locked. | 9 | **Payoff and ending** | 11:00–12:00 | 2010, the 80th anniversary: the National Trust launches a mailboat with 7 postcards. ## Sensitivity & accuracy rules for this story
- **Infant deaths (neonatal tetanus):** 56 births, only 15 survived (1855–76). This is real and important, but heavy. One respectful sentence, no jokes, no graphic images (an empty cradle at most). It's optional to include.
- **Dogs drowned at the evacuation:** documented, but it would wreck the tone. **Leave it out** (not misleading; it isn't part of our through-line).
- **Living and recent people:** the Norway children and their grandfather appear stylised and unnamed, doing only what's documented (finding the boat, posting the cards). Prince Charles is shown only through his letter text, not depicted as acting.
- **Reconstructions:** all 1877–1930 scenes are artistic reconstructions. No invented dialogue. Characters only say documented quotes, or say nothing.
- **Humour targets:** situations, tourists, bureaucracy and the ocean. Never the islanders' poverty or deaths.
