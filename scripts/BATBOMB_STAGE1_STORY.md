### Our fresh angle
Nearly every existing video tells the Wikipedia version:
1. A dentist writes to FDR.
2. The bats burn down a base.
3. The project is cancelled.

We tell it as a **comedy of errors, scene by scene, using the participants' own first-hand accounts**:
- Chemist Louis Fieser's memoir, via Robert Neer's *Napalm: An American Biography* (Harvard UP, 2013)
- Teen bat-scientist Jack Couffer's book *Bat Bomb* (1992)
- Capt. Carr's 1943 Army report

Most people have never heard these details:
- A **dinner party with guests** thrown for a top-secret weapons test.
- **150 bats instead of 3,000.**
- A bomber full of "shrieking, kicking bats".
- **Toy-company timers and a bomb casing made by Bing Crosby's company.**
- A rancher who swore secrecy "even by puttin' bamboo splinters under my fingernails".
- Guards who **wouldn't unlock the gate for the fire trucks.**
- An officer who then **asked the base commander for a bulldozer.**

The ending is ironic but not a moral: the man who wanted to drop fire from planes ended up dropping **grass seed** from planes.

## A. Video concept
**Title options:**
1. **Six Bats Burned Down a US Air Base**
2. **A Dentist Convinced the President to Bomb Japan With Bats**
3. **America's Secret Bat Bomb Worked. That Was the Problem.**

**Central storyline.** In 1942 a cheerful dentist-inventor convinces Franklin Roosevelt to fund an air force of bats carrying napalm. A ragtag team of scientists, teenagers, a lobster fisherman and a former mob driver spends two years and $2 million trying to make it work. Every fix creates a new disaster, until the bats finally work perfectly, on the wrong target.

**Audience:** 16–45, fans of Oversimplified, Sam O'Nella, Fat Electrician, Simple History and weird-WWII content. Mostly US and UK.

**Why it's interesting:** "the government seriously did this" plus escalating chaos. Every act ends worse than the last.

**Thumbnail:**
- A small, cute, slightly worried stylised bat flies toward the camera with a tiny capsule strapped to its chest.
- Behind it, an airfield control tower is burning like a candle against a blue New Mexico sky.
- Text: **"15 MINUTES"**. Optional: a red circle on the bat's capsule.

**Recurring visual identity:** one expressive "hero bat" in close-ups, with no dialogue. It's our running gag and thumbnail mascot. Everything else is real people and places.

## B. Story structure: 9 beats

| # | Beat | Time | What happens (sourced) | Key cinematic shots | Humour / tone |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook** | 0:00–0:30 | May 1943, a brand-new US airfield in New Mexico. A chemist injects six tiny timers in front of Army cameramen. The bats wake in the hot sun and fly off. *"Exactly 15 minutes after arming, a barracks burst into flames; minutes later the tall tower erupted into a huge candle."* (Couffer) Fire trucks arrive, and the guards refuse to open the gate. | Bats lifting off, a clock ticking, the barracks igniting, the tower burning, fire trucks stopped at a locked gate | Maya: "To understand how six bats beat the US Army, we have to start with a dentist." |
| 2 | **The dentist** | 0:30–2:00 | Lytle S. "Doc" Adams: dentist, pilot and inventor from Irwin, PA. He invented a system that let planes snatch mailbags without landing, and took Eleanor Roosevelt on a demo flight. In Dec 1941 he visits Carlsbad Caverns, watches millions of bats pour out at dusk, and hears about Pearl Harbor on the way home. | Mail plane hooking a bag, Adams with Eleanor in a cockpit, the dusk bat flight out of Carlsbad (the big spectacle shot), a car radio | Light. Establish him as jolly and unstoppable |
| 3 | **The letter** | 2:00–3:15 | 12 Jan 1942, letter to FDR: bats, "the lowest form of life… were placed there by God to await this hour." FDR to the OSS chief: **"This man is not a nut. It sounds like a perfectly wild idea but is worth looking into."** | Adams typing, the letter traveling to the White House, FDR reading and scribbling the note | **Ryan cutaway #1:** "The President wrote 'not a nut'? That's the bar?" |
| 4 | **Building it** | 3:15–5:15 | The team: mammalogist von Bloeker, high-schooler Jack Couffer, a lobster fisherman and a former mob getaway driver. *"He chose them more for personality than technical expertise"* (Couffer). Adams claimed they searched about 1,000 caves and 3,000 mines. Ney Cave, Texas, had 20–30 million bats, which took 5 hours to fly out. The free-tailed bat (about 9–11 g) can carry 15–18 g. Napalm's inventor, **Louis Fieser** (Harvard), designs a 17.5 g napalm capsule; a chemical eats through a wire to set off the timer. Toy-maker **A.C. Gilbert** (the Erector Set company) makes timers. **Bing Crosby's company** builds the bomb shells, each with about 1,030 bats in egg-crate trays. The plan: drop at dawn, parachute at 4,000 ft, bats roost in attics, thousands of fires at once. | Montage: the oddball team recruited, the cave river of bats, nets, a chemistry lab, a toy factory, a bomb shell cutaway diagram (animated explainer) | Fast, funny montage. **Ryan cutaway #2:** "Bing Crosby?!" |
| 5 | **Test 1: Muroc, California** | 5:15–6:45 | Fieser arrives for the top-secret test (17 May 1943). Adams has thrown **a dinner party with guests** to celebrate. He has **150 bats instead of 3,000** ("mating season"). An emergency B-25 flight brings crates from Carlsbad: *"loaded with shrieking, kicking bats."* They're chilled overnight with ice and fans. Next day the cardboard mock-up bombs fall apart. The pilot refuses live incendiaries, so the bats get weights and are thrown out by hand while jeeps chase them across the lakebed. They drop like stones. Fieser: *"we had frozen them to death the night before."* | Dinner party with candles, the bomber full of bats, ice blocks and fans, cardboard bomb disintegrating, jeeps racing on a white lakebed | Peak comedy, but keep the bat deaths **non-graphic** (cut away from impact) |
| 6 | **Test 2: Carlsbad, NM** | 6:45–8:15 | A brand-new auxiliary airfield. The base commander, a full colonel, agrees to delay its opening, and the lieutenant colonel running the test **bars him from watching** for secrecy. A steel shell drops, the chute opens at 4,000 ft, and *"tiny motes began to flutter across the sky."* Jeeps chase them miles to a ranch. Rancher: *"Like bats flyin' 'round in broad daylight?"* He swears secrecy (bamboo splinters line). There's a bat on his porch rafters with a dummy bomb. **It worked.** | Parachute bloom, a sky full of bats, a jeep chase over desert, the rancher on his porch, close-up of the hero bat under the eaves | Triumph beat, with the rancher as comic relief |
| 7 | **Turning point: the fire** | 8:15–9:45 | Adams wants one more test. Fieser wants to impress the Army cameramen: six bats with live 15-minute timers. The sun wakes them instantly and they fly into the brand-new base. Barracks, tower, offices and hangars burn *"in order corresponding to the intervals between Fieser's injections."* There's no fire equipment on site (secrecy). The commander arrives with three fire engines; the guards won't unlock the gate. The officer in charge then **asks the commander for a bulldozer** to clear what's left. Air Force Magazine (1990) adds that **a general's car** burned too. **The Army quits.** | The full hook sequence, now in context: syringe, clock, sequential fires, fire trucks at the gate, the bulldozer request | The biggest laugh of the film. **Ryan cutaway #3:** silent stare |
| 8 | **Second life, then climax** | 9:45–11:00 | Marine General Louis DeHaven watched the test and was impressed. The Navy and Marines take over and rename it **Project X-Ray**. Marines guard the bat caves, and Adams is pushed out. Dec 1943, Dugway, Utah: mock Japanese and German villages. Testers conclude the bats were effective, and Fieser estimates 3.7× more efficient than ordinary firebombs. Design is done by Feb 1944, with factories ready for 1 million bats. **Then it's cancelled.** Admiral King hears it won't be combat-ready until mid-1945. Total cost: about $2 million. The exact reason is still debated (Neer calls it unresolved). Adams later recalled a general confusing his project with *"another secret project… the silliest nonsense you ever heard of"*, which was the atomic bomb. | Marines at cave mouths, the Dugway mock village, the cancellation telegram, a desk stamp | Tension, then deflation. **Ryan cutaway #4:** "So they *won*… and lost?" |
| 9 | **Aftermath and ending** | 11:00–12:00 | **One respectful sentence:** in March 1945, conventional firebombing of Tokyo destroyed much of the city and killed over 100,000 people. No jokes here. Then the epilogue: teenager Jack Couffer became a Hollywood cinematographer, and in 1946 Adams flew over Arizona **scattering grass-seed pellets from a plane** to regrow rangeland. | Quiet ember-to-dawn transition, then Adams in a plane dropping seed pellets, green shoots | Last line (dry, not a moral): *"He finally found something to drop from a plane that grew instead of burned."* Ryan/Maya tag |

**Runtime check:** about 1,750 words at Maya's pace (about 155 wpm) is about 11.3 minutes, plus about 45 s of silent cinematic beats (bat flight, fire sequence). That gives roughly 12 minutes, with mid-roll room after beats 4 and 7.

## Sensitivity and tone rules
- **The target was Japanese cities full of civilians.** We never joke about the intended victims, and we never show Japanese people as comic targets. The jokes are about the bureaucracy, the chaos and Adams's optimism.
- **Animal deaths** (frozen bats): no graphic impacts. We cut to the jeeps and to Fieser's dry quote.
- **The Tokyo sentence** gets silence, a music drop and no Ryan.
- **Real people** (FDR, Eleanor, Fieser, Adams, DeHaven, King) are stylised and respectful. They only say documented quotes; otherwise they're silent or act out documented events.
- **Reconstructions** are labelled as such in the description. No invented dialogue.
