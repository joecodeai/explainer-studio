# Stage 2: Narration. "Six Bats Burned Down a US Air Base"
*Draft 1, 21 Nov 2026. Maya narrates; Ryan cuts in 5 times as the audience's reaction.*

**Format of each line:** `SPEAKER [voice tag]: text`
- **Voice tags** come from `library/voices/maya|benny/delivery.json`.
- **Never a pause inside a sentence.** Gaps between lines are marked `(beat)` only where a picture fills them.
- **Quotes:** every direct quote is a real, sourced quote, read by Maya as narration ("he wrote"). No historical character speaks invented words, and no lip-synced historical dialogue.

**Sound rule (from you):** no background music bed. Sound is natural ambience and foley. Where music appears at all, it's *source* music inside the scene (a gramophone at the dinner party) or a single sustained tone at the one serious moment.

**Running gag:** Roosevelt's verdict, "This man is not a nut." It's called back three times as things escalate, and paid off at the end of beat 8. It never touches the deaths.

## 1 · COLD OPEN (0:00–0:35)
*Sound: hot wind, a fly buzzing, a camera shutter whirr. The syringe's tiny glass click.*

MAYA [narration]: In the spring of 1943, a Harvard chemist stood on a brand-new American airfield and armed six sleeping bats with tiny firebombs, while Army cameramen filmed him doing it.

MAYA [dry]: The desert sun woke the bats up before anyone had planned for it.

`(beat 2.5 s: SILENT, six bats lift off a tray and scatter over the new buildings. Sound: wingbeats, then only wind.)`

MAYA [narration]: Exactly fifteen minutes later, the barracks caught fire. Then the control tower went up like a giant candle. Then the offices, and then the hangars, one after another, in the order he had armed the bats.

MAYA [deadpan]: The fire trucks raced to the gate, and the guards refused to open it, because the whole thing was top secret.

`(beat 1.5 s: fire trucks idling at a locked gate, smoke behind. Sound: idling engines, distant crackle.)`

MAYA [narration]: To understand how six bats beat the United States Army, we have to go back to a dentist.

`TITLE CARD: SIX BATS BURNED DOWN A US AIR BASE. Sound: one match strike, then silence.`

## 2 · THE DENTIST (0:35–1:55)
*Sound: a small-town dental office (a ticking clock, a drill whine far off), then propeller drone, then the desert at dusk with insects and the roar of wings.*

MAYA [narration]: Lytle Adams was a dentist from Irwin, Pennsylvania, which is a strange way to begin a war story. He was also a pilot and an inventor.

MAYA [amused]: His big idea before the war was a hook that let a plane snatch a sack of mail off the ground without ever landing. He'd once taken Eleanor Roosevelt up on a demonstration flight, which will matter in a minute.

MAYA [narration]: In December 1941, Adams was on his way home from Carlsbad Caverns in New Mexico, where every evening a river of bats pours out of the cave and into the sky.

`(beat 4 s: SILENT, the Carlsbad dusk bat flight, crane up the column. Sound: a rising hiss of wings, no music.)`

MAYA [narration]: Then the radio told him about Pearl Harbor.

MAYA [dry]: Most people heard that news and felt fear, or anger. Doc Adams looked at the bats.

MAYA [narration]: Years later he described the moment himself. He had been, in his words, "tremendously impressed by the bat flight," and he wondered: "Couldn't those millions of bats be fitted with incendiary bombs and dropped from planes?"

---

## 3 · THE LETTER (1:55–3:00)
*Sound: typewriter keys and a carriage return, an envelope sliding, the hush of a big office, a fountain pen scratch.*

MAYA [narration]: On the twelfth of January, 1942, he wrote to the President of the United States. Through his connection to the First Lady, the letter actually reached him.

MAYA [dry]: It opened gently. The bat, Adams wrote, was "the lowest form of life," and the reasons for its creation had always remained a mystery, until now. Bats, he explained, had been "placed there by God to await this hour."

MAYA [narration]: His plan was to catch bats by the million, strap a small firebomb to each one, and release them over Japan before dawn. Bats look for dark places to roost, under eaves and in attics, and most Japanese homes at the time were built of wood and paper. The timers would go off, and thousands of fires would start in places no one could see.

MAYA [narration]: Franklin Roosevelt read it, and passed it to his intelligence chief, William Donovan, with a note that still survives: "This man is not a nut. It sounds like a perfectly wild idea but is worth looking into."

`RYAN CUTAWAY #1 (studio, single, 5 s)`
RYAN [disbelief]: That's the reference? "Not a nut"?

MAYA [dry]: From the President. Most of us can't get that from our own families.

`INSIGHT 1: why it didn't sound crazy (context and analysis)`
MAYA [narration]: It sounds absurd now, but look at it from January 1942. America had just watched its Pacific battleships sunk or wrecked at Pearl Harbor, and it had no way to reach Japan at all. The first American air raid on Tokyo was still three months away. A cheap, quiet weapon that fit in your hand didn't sound crazy. It sounded like an option.

## 4 · BUILDING A BAT AIR FORCE (3:00–5:05)
*Sound: car on a dirt road (a battered Buick), cave drips and echo, the dense roar of a colony leaving, lab glassware, a toy-factory clatter, sheet metal being riveted.*

MAYA [narration]: Armed with that note, Adams went recruiting. His team included a mammal scientist from a Los Angeles museum, a high-school student named Jack Couffer, who had turned seventeen on the day of Pearl Harbor, a lobster fisherman, and a man who had driven getaway cars for the mob.

MAYA [amused]: Couffer later admitted that Adams "chose them more for personality than technical expertise."

MAYA [narration]: They drove across the Southwest looking for the perfect bat. Adams claimed they visited a thousand caves and three thousand mines. In Ney Cave in Texas, the Army found a colony of twenty to thirty million Mexican free-tailed bats, so many that, according to an Army report, "five hours' time is required for these animals to leave the cave."

`(beat 3 s: SILENT, wide shot of the Texas cave at dusk, a dark ribbon of bats unrolling across the sky, the team's car tiny below.)`

MAYA [narration]: The free-tailed bat weighs about a third of an ounce, and in tests it could fly carrying more than its own weight. For the bombs, the government brought in Louis Fieser, the Harvard chemist who had just invented napalm.

MAYA [narration]: Fieser built a firebomb the size of a fingertip, with a timer that worked by letting a chemical slowly eat through a thin wire. When the wire broke, the bomb ignited.

MAYA [dry]: The timers were made by the A. C. Gilbert company, which also made the Erector Set. The bomb casings were built by a company owned by the singer Bing Crosby and his brother.

`RYAN CUTAWAY #2 (studio, single, 5 s)`
RYAN [deadpan]: So the team is a dentist, a toy company and Bing Crosby.

MAYA [dry]: And Harvard. To keep it serious.

MAYA [narration]: Here's how the finished weapon was supposed to work. Each casing held about a thousand chilled, sleeping bats in stacked trays, like eggs in a carton. A bomber drops it, a parachute opens, the casing falls away, and the bats wake up as they fall into warmer air and scatter for up to twenty miles in every direction. They hide in the rafters, and then the timers go off.

`EXPLAINER ANIMATION (Remotion, ~10 s, under the line above): casing, parachute, trays separating, bats dispersing, roofs, timer wire dissolving.`

## 5 · TEST ONE: THE PARTY (5:05–6:40)
*Sound: crickets outside a house; inside, clinking glasses, laughter and a gramophone (the only music, playing in the room). Then B-25 engines on a desert strip, muffled shrieking from crates, the hum of a refrigeration truck, electric fans, wind over the lakebed, jeeps racing.*

MAYA [narration]: In May 1943 it was time for the first real test, at Muroc Army Air Base in the California desert. Louis Fieser flew in with two Army officers for a quiet evening briefing on a top-secret weapon.

MAYA [dry]: Fieser later wrote, "We were horrified to find that Adams had invited a large company, including ladies, to a dinner party in celebration of the initiation of field tests."

`RYAN CUTAWAY #3 (studio, single, 4 s)`
RYAN [awkward]: He threw a party. For the secret weapon.

MAYA [deadpan]: It was very secret. Only the guests knew.

MAYA [narration]: There was a second problem. The plan called for three thousand bats. Adams had caught a hundred and fifty. It was mating season, he explained, and they were hard to catch.

MAYA [narration]: So the next morning a B-25 bomber flew to Carlsbad, loaded up crates of bats, and flew straight back. In Fieser's words, "Shortly after dinner the bomber flew in loaded with shrieking, kicking bats."

`(beat 2.5 s: SILENT, the B-25 landing at dusk, crates shaking in the bomb bay. Sound: engines winding down, then the shrieking.)`

MAYA [narration]: They packed the bats into a refrigerated truck to put them into hibernation, then added blocks of ice wrapped in towels, then fans. Around midnight, the shrieking finally stopped.

MAYA [narration]: The next morning, nothing went to plan. The cardboard test bombs fell apart the moment they left the plane. The pilot refused to carry live firebombs, so the team clipped small weights to the bats instead and threw them out of the bomber by hand, while jeeps chased across the dry lakebed below.

MAYA [narration]: The bats fell straight down. They climbed higher and tried again, with the same result.

MAYA [tired]: Fieser worked out why. "Instead of freezing them to hibernation, we had frozen them to death the night before."

`(beat 1.5 s: the jeeps slowing to a stop on the white lakebed, dust drifting. Sound: engines idling, wind. No joke here.)`

## 6 · TEST TWO: IT WORKS (6:40–8:00)
*Sound: a quieter desert, larks, a steel casing creaking on a bomb rack, a parachute snapping open, a soft rush of wings, jeeps over rough ground, a screen door, porch boards creaking.*

MAYA [narration]: For the second test, the team moved close to the bat caves, to a brand-new auxiliary airfield at Carlsbad. It had a fresh control tower, barracks, offices and hangars, and none of it had been used yet.

MAYA [dry]: The base commander, a full colonel, agreed to delay opening his own airfield for the secret project. The lieutenant colonel running the test then barred him from watching, for secrecy. Still not a nut, technically.

MAYA [narration]: This time the bats went into a real steel casing, carrying dummy bombs. The bomber dropped it, and the parachute opened.

`(beat 3 s: SILENT, parachute blooms over the desert, the casing opens, a cloud of bats spills out. Sound: the parachute crack, then a soft rush of wings.)`

MAYA [narration]: Jack Couffer watched as "tiny motes began to flutter across the sky." The team leapt into jeeps and chased them for miles, to a ranch house. They asked the old rancher on the porch if he'd noticed anything unusual.

MAYA [amused]: He had. "Like bats flyin' 'round in broad daylight? Unusual like that?"

MAYA [narration]: They begged him to keep it secret. He had two sons fighting in Europe, he told them, and nobody would get a word out of him, "even by puttin' bamboo splinters under my fingernails."

MAYA [narration]: Then he pointed up. Tucked between the boards of his porch ceiling sat a bat, still wearing its dummy bomb.

`(beat 2 s: SILENT, the rancher looking up, the hero bat blinking down. Sound: porch creak, a single wing rustle.)`

MAYA [narration]: The delivery system worked.

## 7 · THE FIRE (8:00–9:35)
*Sound: camera shutters and the whirr of film cameras, the syringe clicks, wingbeats, then a long stretch of hot wind and silence, then fire (crackle building to a roar, glass bursting, a tower groaning), sirens arriving, engines idling at the gate, two men arguing out of earshot.*

MAYA [narration]: That would have been a very good place to stop. Instead, Adams wanted a second run to confirm the results, and Fieser saw a chance to show his invention to the Army Signal Corps cameramen.

MAYA [narration]: He took six chilled bats with live bombs attached, and one by one, for the cameras, he injected the chemical into their timers. The timers were set for fifteen minutes.

MAYA [dry]: That's when the New Mexico sun woke the bats up.

`(beat 2 s: SILENT, the six bats lifting off, Fieser's startled face, the bats flying toward the new buildings.)`

MAYA [narration]: Couffer remembered exactly what happened next: "Exactly fifteen minutes after arming, a barracks burst into flames; minutes later the tall tower erupted into a huge candle visible for miles."

`(beat 3 s: SILENT, the control tower fire, push in. Sound: the fire's roar takes over. Nothing else.)`

MAYA [narration]: The offices and hangars followed, in the same order Fieser had armed the bats. Because the test was only supposed to use dummy bombs, and because it was secret, no one had brought any firefighting equipment.

MAYA [narration]: The base commander, who was still not allowed in, saw the smoke and arrived with three fire engines. The guards at the gate refused to unlock it.

MAYA [deadpan]: Once the fires had burned out, the officer in charge asked the commander if he could borrow a bulldozer, to flatten whatever was left.

MAYA [narration]: Some accounts say even a general's car caught fire. The Army's official report summed up the day in one sentence: testing was concluded "when a fire destroyed a large portion of the test material."

`RYAN CUTAWAY #4 (studio, single, 4 s, SILENT): Ryan just stares at the camera. No line.`

MAYA [dry]: That's one way to describe an airfield.

MAYA [narration]: The Army decided it wanted nothing more to do with the bats.

`INSIGHT 2: what the test really showed (our analysis)`
MAYA [dry]: And that's the part most retellings miss. The test didn't show the bats failing. It showed them working, on the wrong target. Every bat went exactly where bats go, somewhere dark, warm and wooden. That was the whole idea, and the whole problem. You can't aim a bat.

## 8 · SECOND LIFE, AND THE END (9:35–11:00)
*Sound: Marine drill commands in the distance, wind at a cave mouth, a chain-link gate, then the empty quiet of the Utah desert, the crackle of small fires in a mock village, a typewriter, a rubber stamp.*

MAYA [narration]: But one man in the crowd that day had been impressed: Marine Corps General Louis DeHaven. The Navy took the project over, gave it a proper code name, Project X-Ray, and handed it to the Marines.

MAYA [narration]: The Marines posted armed guards at the biggest bat caves in Texas. They also eased out the man who had started it all. Doc Adams was off his own project.

MAYA [narration]: In December 1943, at the Dugway Proving Ground in Utah, the Marines tested the bats against full-size replica Japanese and German buildings. This time they were careful. The bats carried dummies, and the team placed real firebombs wherever the bats had landed.

`(beat 2.5 s: SILENT, the mock village under grey winter sky, small fires starting under eaves. Sound: fire crackle, wind.)`

MAYA [narration]: The results were good. Dugway's chief of incendiary testing concluded the bats could plant bombs inside buildings without anyone noticing. Fieser calculated they were about three times more effective than ordinary firebombs. By early 1944 the design was finished, and suppliers stood ready to produce a million of them.

MAYA [narration]: And then the project was cancelled. The Navy's top admiral, Ernest King, heard it wouldn't be ready for combat until the middle of 1945, and stopped it. A government chemist pointed to "uncertainties involved in the behavior of the animal." Historians still disagree about the real reason. The bill came to about two million dollars.

`RYAN CUTAWAY #5 (studio, single, 6 s)`
RYAN [deflated]: Two years, two million dollars, it finally works, and that's when they cancel it.

MAYA [dry]: The bats were always right on time. The paperwork wasn't.

`INSIGHT 3: reading the sources side by side (research and our read)`
MAYA [narration]: Read the accounts side by side and they don't even agree on the details: the date of the fire, whose car burned, why it was stopped. Our read is that the bats never ran out of promise. They ran out of time, because the project went from the Army, to the Navy, to the Marines, and every handoff started the clock again.

MAYA [narration]: Doc Adams never really understood why. Couffer later remembered Adams coming back from Washington, baffled, because a general had mixed up their secret project with another one he'd never heard of. Adams called it "the silliest nonsense you ever heard of."

MAYA [narration]: The other project was the atomic bomb.

MAYA [dry]: Roosevelt had been right, in the end. Doc Adams was not a nut. He was something far more dangerous: a cheerful man with a letter from the President in his briefcase.

## 9 · AFTERMATH (11:00–11:55)
*Sound: for the first line, one low sustained tone and distant wind, nothing else. Then light returns: a small plane's engine over open country, seed pellets pattering, birdsong. Last: the dusk roar of bats leaving a cave, fading out.*

MAYA [narration]: The idea of burning Japan's wooden cities didn't end with the bats. In March 1945, American bombers firebombed Tokyo with ordinary incendiaries. In a single night, about a hundred thousand people were killed.

`(beat 3 s: SILENT, embers fading to a dark screen. No Ryan, no joke.)`

MAYA [narration]: The bat team went their separate ways. Jack Couffer, the teenage bat scientist, grew up to be an Oscar-nominated Hollywood cinematographer.

MAYA [narration]: And in 1946, Doc Adams went back up in a plane, with government backing, and scattered thousands of seed pellets over the Arizona desert, trying to grow grass on worn-out land.

MAYA [dry]: After years of trying to drop fire from the sky, he'd finally found something to drop that grew instead of burned.

`(beat 4 s: SILENT, dusk at Carlsbad, the bat river rising again, the hero bat peeling off alone and flying into the dark. Sound: wings, crickets. Cut to black.)`
