# HUMOUR AND SCRIPTWRITING STYLE GUIDE (from the user, 2026-11-23; replaces the 2026-11-18 guide, kept as HUMOR_GUIDE_2026-11-18.md)

The channel is a funny, entertaining history/education channel. The biggest priority is HUMOUR. Not conventional educational
narration with occasional jokes: the narration itself must be entertaining.

## 1. The humour
Study (never copy wording, catchphrases or voices): Casually Explained (PRIMARY), Sam O'Nella Academy, History Matters, Ryan George, CGP Grey.
Dry and sarcastic. Conversational and effortless. Occasionally dark and absurd. Clever without sounding intellectual. Naturally funny
rather than deliberately comedic. Slightly disrespectful towards ridiculous historical decisions. Built on observations, irony,
contradictions and unexpected comparisons.
Imagine a very intelligent, slightly sarcastic friend explaining something ridiculous. The narrator isn't trying to be a comedian;
they tell you what happened and occasionally point out how unbelievably stupid, ironic or absurd it was. Almost bored by how stupid it is.

## 2. Don't force jokes (the biggest problem with AI humour)
BAD: "Parliament was shocked. The sewage was not impressed." / "London had a problem. And that problem was... poop!" /
"They tried everything. Spoiler alert: nothing worked."
BETTER: "Thousands of people were dying. Parliament had been discussing the problem for years. Apparently, the smell was what finally made it urgent."
The user's examples of the right voice:
- "Thousands of people were dying. Parliament decided the obvious solution was to schedule another meeting."
- "They then tried throwing lime into the Thames. Which was essentially putting deodorant on a corpse."
Humour comes from the observation, not an obvious punchline. Jokes aren't announced. A little attitude. The audience discovers it.

## 3. Techniques (vary them)
A dry sarcasm (ridiculous thing, normal tone) - B historical irony (what they believed vs what was happening) - C understatement
(disaster described as a minor inconvenience) - D unexpected comparisons to ordinary modern situations - E casual insults at terrible
decisions, incompetent leaders, ridiculous traditions - F conversational asides that sound spontaneous - G escalation - H callbacks (only when natural).

## 4. Writing and narration
Like speaking to a friend: short punchy sentences, natural transitions, occasional rhetorical questions, unexpected sentence endings,
varied rhythm, occasional pauses for timing (between sentences only, never inside one).
Avoid: dramatic documentary narration, exclamation marks, internet slang, memes, obvious setups, explaining the joke, every sentence a punchline.

## 5. Density
NOT every line a joke. Some sentences just deliver information so the next observation lands harder.
Rough rhythm FACT -> OBSERVATION -> STORY -> SARCASTIC COMMENT -> FACT -> UNEXPECTED JOKE, never applied mechanically.
The audience should never feel they're waiting for the next pre-written joke.

## 6. Visual humour
Narration and animation complement each other. Sometimes funny narration over a serious visual; sometimes normal narration while the
animation delivers the joke. Never the same joke in both. Visual exaggeration, awkward pauses, ridiculous expressions, unexpected background details.

## 7. Accuracy
Comedy never compromises accuracy: dates, names, statistics, events, cause and effect stay correct. Never invent dialogue or events.
Exaggerate the presentation of a real event, never the facts.

## 8. Pre-delivery check (every sentence)
Real person talking? Genuinely funny or just shaped like a joke? Clever or predictable sarcasm? Trying too hard? Can it be shorter and
funnier? Does it fit the story? Funny without exaggerated delivery? If forced: rewrite or remove. If already funny: don't add another joke.

GOLDEN RULE: effortless. Not a comedian performing jokes, not a presenter trying to be funny: an incredibly entertaining person casually
explaining something ridiculous that actually happened. They laugh at how the narrator sees it, not because the script tells them to.

## 9. No repetitive, predictable or cringe humour
Never reuse the same sarcastic structures, setups/punchlines, comparison types, insults, reactions, transitions, sentence endings or techniques.
Banned patterns (and their variations): "Which was... not ideal." / "Apparently, that didn't work." / "Things were about to get worse." /
"Because obviously, that made sense." / "And somehow, it gets worse." / "The [thing] was not impressed." / "What could possibly go wrong?" /
"Spoiler: it didn't work." Also no "Step one / step two" list formulas.
No memes, Gen Z slang, random app/tech references, forced pop culture, cheap toilet humour, obvious one-liners, overdramatic reactions,
typical ChatGPT humour. Absurdity is not randomness; jokes must make sense in context.
Vary the approach; sometimes just state the absurd fact and add nothing. Don't use the same technique in consecutive paragraphs.
Trust the audience: never explain a joke, never repeat it in other words, never add a sarcastic line after an already funny one,
never turn every fact into a punchline, let moments breathe.

**Every script gets its own comedic personality.** Don't develop a comedy formula and apply it to every story. Let the subject decide
the humour (a disastrous invention, a ridiculous king, a failed expedition and an accidental discovery should each feel different).
Never let the audience predict the next joke.

Final check: repeated structures? repeated sarcastic expressions? predictable punchlines? forced/childish/cringe? two jokes making the
same observation? generic jokes that could be more specific? jokes that don't improve the story? If a line sounds like something an AI
comedy writer would predictably generate, reconsider it.
